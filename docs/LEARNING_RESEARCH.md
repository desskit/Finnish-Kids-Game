# Learning audit v3: what the research says, and how the app measures up

*Written October 2026. The audit had three parts:*
- *a survey of second-language research for children aged 4–10;*
- *a survey of guidance on teaching Finnish to English-speaking children;*
- *a map of how the app teaches today.*

*The app is for readers aged 6–10:*
- *"Younger" (about 6–7) get a lighter touch.*
- *"Older" (about 8–10) keep the explicit lessons.*

*Spoken Finnish is taught for recognition only. Audio stays on browser speech for now. The phased plan is at the end; earlier audits are in [LEARNING_AUDIT.md](LEARNING_AUDIT.md).*

**Strength labels:**
- **[Strong]**: a meta-analysis, official guidance, or several studies that agree.
- **[Moderate]**: a few good studies, or practitioner consensus.
- **[Tentative]**: one study, a small or funded sample, or an inference.

Most sources were read as abstracts or summaries. Check any figure against the original before quoting it.

## 1. Children learning a second language (ages 4–10)

### Input before rules

- **[Strong]** Starting younger only pays off with a lot of input. In the Barcelona Age Factor project (Muñoz), children who started at 8 were overtaken by later starters after 100–700 hours of lessons. The young starters' only edge was in listening.
  - Young children learn mostly *implicitly*. The amount and quality of input matter more than explanation.
- **[Moderate]** Children start with whole memorised phrases (*quel âge as-tu?*), then break them apart. That breaking apart drives grammar (Myles, Mitchell & Hooper).
  - Teach useful phrases as wholes. Later, vary one slot at a time.
- **[Moderate]** Leave out rule talk for the youngest. By about 8–9 years old, a short "notice the pattern" moment works, such as a coloured ending. Give it a light touch, not a lesson (Spada & Tomita; Cameron).
- **[Moderate]** Use predictable routines that grow more complex (Cameron, *Teaching Languages to Young Learners*).

### Vocabulary: exposures, spacing, retrieval

- **[Strong]** Repetition predicts learning, but there's no magic number (Uchihara, Webb & Yanagisawa 2019).
  - Plan for about 6–10 meaningful meetings before a word is understood.
  - Plan for about 15–20 retrieval chances before production is expected.
  - Spread these over sessions and contexts.
- **[Strong in adults, moderate in children]** Spacing beats massing.
  - For a test a week away, the best gap is about 20–40% of the delay (Cepeda 2008).
  - Testing during spaced practice adds to the benefit (Kang 2016).
  - Even 3-year-olds generalise better with breaks between examples (Vlach 2008).
  - A practical pattern: recall the same day after other items, then about 1, 3 and 7 days later, then 2–3 weeks.
- **[Moderate, mixed in classrooms]** Retrieval helps 5-year-olds most when something comes between learning and recall.
  - It helped with intervening items in Leonard 2019 and Haebig 2021, in blocks of about 10 minutes.
  - It made no difference inside storybooks (Knabe 2023).
  - So don't break stories up with quizzes. Recall is better than multiple choice where the child can manage it.
- **[Moderate, debated]** Teaching close neighbours together (cat, dog, mouse; all the colours) causes interference. Theme sets, the things in one scene, do no worse (Tinkham; Waring).
  - Add members of a category one or two at a time.
- **[Moderate]** Show picture + audio first, then the word in a sentence and a scene, then the written form.

### Listening, speaking and stories

- **[Moderate]** Hearing *the same* story three times taught 3-year-olds more words than three different stories (Horst, Parsons & Bryan 2011).
  - Give each unit one core story and replay it.
- **[Moderate]** Acting a word out with gestures (TPR) helps memory (Macedonia 2014, including 5–7 year-olds).
- **[Moderate in adults, tentative in children]** Singing helps recall of phrases (Ludke 2014). For children the main gain reported is confidence and repetition.
- **[Strong for infants, inferred for older]** Children learn sounds from live people, not from audio alone (Kuhl 2003).
  - A native voice of good quality matters, and so does interaction.
- **[Strong on the limits]** Speech recognition gets 11–35% of children's words wrong.
  - Never mark pronunciation wrong because the recogniser says so. Use it to encourage, let a parent judge, or let the child compare their recording with the model.

### Reading

- **[Moderate]** Introduce reading after speech. Reading is optional and later in each unit, after the word is known by ear.
- **[Strong]** In shallow spelling systems like Finnish, children read accurately by the end of first grade. English takes about twice as long (Seymour, Aro & Erskine 2003).
  - Children who already read English can decode Finnish fast.
  - Watch for carry-over from English (*y*, *ä*, *j*).

### Motivation, rewards and feedback

- **[Strong]** Rewards that are expected for doing tasks reduce later free-choice motivation (Deci, Koestner & Ryan 1999, 128 studies; Lepper 1973).
  - Unexpected rewards, and rewards that tell the child what they've learned, don't.
  - Prefer surprises, rewards tied to content, and "you learned 5 words".
- **[Moderate]** Badges and leaderboards bring a novelty boost that fades. There should be no leaderboards for young children.
- **[Strong]** Extras unrelated to a story *hurt* learning; story-related animation helps (Takacs, Swart & Bus 2015).
- **[Moderate]** Praise effort and strategy. After an error say "listen again" and replay the audio. No harsh sounds and no lost lives.
- **[Tentative]** Keep sessions short: about 10–15 minutes at 6–7 and 12–20 at 8–10, with a natural stopping point. Several short sessions beat one long one.

### Parents and social interaction

- **[Strong for ages 0–3, inferred for older]** Live interaction gates language learning. Toddlers learned verbs from live or responsive video chat, but not from recorded video (Kuhl; Roseberry 2014).
- **[Moderate]** Parents who ask questions during screen time raise story vocabulary (Strouse 2013), and their modelling helps too.
- **[Moderate]** Device-heavy e-books shift parent talk to the device (Parish-Morris 2013).
- **[Moderate]** Parent engagement is worth about +3 months of progress, and up to +5 in the early years (EEF).
- Design: a "together" mode with short prompt cards for the grown-up. It needs no fluent Finnish, links the screen to the real world, and is optional.

### Assessment and the "four pillars"

- **[Moderate]** CEFR young-learner can-do descriptors give a pre-A1/A1 frame for progress.
  - Children judge themselves best right after a task (Butler & Lee). Ask them then, and show the grown-up the can-dos, not scores.
- **[Strong framework]** Hirsh-Pasek et al. (2015) set four pillars for learning from apps: *active*, *engaged*, *meaningful*, *socially interactive*.
  - Most top apps score low, with "social" often 0 (Meyer 2021).
- **[Tentative]** No independent trial of a gamified second-language app for children aged 4–10 was found. Duolingo ABC's figure comes from a study the company commissioned.

## 2. Finnish for English-speaking children

### Official guidance

- **[Strong]** Suomi-koulu curriculum recommendation (Opetushallitus 2015, ages 3–18):
  - Listening and speaking through stories, play, rhymes and songs.
  - Pre-reading through read-alouds and playing with letters and sounds.
  - Themes: greetings, family, home, animals, clothes, food, body, nature and weather, seasons and times of day, toys, then feelings. Days, months, clock times and school subjects at 6–8.
  - Numbers 1–20, then to 100.
  - It moves "from everyday language to the language of thinking".
  - Parents are named as the child's daily, lasting contact with Finnish.
- **[Strong]** Finland's early A1 curriculum (grades 1–2) puts joy in learning, hands-on activity and spoken interaction first. Reading and writing build gradually.
- **[Moderate]** "Language showering" (*kielisuihkutus*) is short, playful exposure with no formal goal, and builds attitudes. Immersion (*kielikylpy*) is much longer exposure.

### Where English speakers struggle

- **[Moderate] Vowel and consonant length** (*tuli / tuuli / tulli*, *kuka / kukka*, *muta / mutta*).
  - English listeners don't hear length as a category. Short explicit training helps.
  - Doubled letters are the most common length error.
  - Give length its own listening track and show it visually.
- **[Moderate] Front vowels ä, ö, y.** Chanting lyrics gave beginners the biggest pronunciation gains (Heikkola & Alisaari 2017).
- **[Moderate] Vowel harmony** (*-ssa / -ssä*). Even native children learn it slowly (Lieko 2013).
  - Never ask a child to choose the vowel. Only ever show correct forms.
- **[Moderate] Numbers take the partitive** (*kaksi koiraa*). Teach number + noun as one chunk.
- **[Tentative] Gradation, possessive suffixes and verb endings.**
  - Finnish children use 3rd-person verb forms early.
  - Delay gradation and possessive suffixes.
  - Possessive suffixes are mostly dropped in speech (*mun kirja*).
- **[Easy wins]** Finnish has no articles and no grammatical gender, and *hän* covers both he and she.

### Order of the cases

- **[Moderate–Strong]** Finnish children under 3 learn the cases in this order (Toivainen 1980):
  1. partitive
  2. adessive
  3. illative
  4. inessive
  5. genitive
  6. ablative
  7. elative
  8. essive
  9. translative
- **[Moderate]** Children in immersion from age 5 followed the native order, but produced the genitive *before* the location cases (Ahonen 2000).
- **[Moderate]** The partitive and the location cases are the most useful for talking (Silfverberg).
- Suggested order:
  1. nominative
  2. partitive as chunks
  3. location cases in *missä / mihin / mistä* pairs
  4. genitive
  5. essive and translative last
- The app's order (adessive → partitive → genitive → location cases) sits within this range. **Kept.**

### Spoken Finnish (puhekieli)

- **[Moderate]** Current teaching (Ahlholm; *Suomen mestari 1*; *Avain suomeen*) brings in everyday spoken Finnish from the first lessons.
- **[Moderate]** An older view (Silfverberg 1993) builds on standard Finnish first.
- **[Strong]** Heritage children hear *mä / sä* at home, and the Suomi-koulu curriculum starts from everyday language.
- Decision: **recognition only.**
  - Children in stories may speak mild spoken Finnish, in lines a native speaker writes.
  - A card shows "two ways to say it" (*minä = mä*).
  - Narration and every drill stay standard Finnish. Spoken forms are never graded.

### Reading Finnish

- **[Strong]** Finland teaches reading with KÄTS (letter → sound → syllable → word). Syllables are the key unit because Finnish words are long.
  - The Suomi-koulu curriculum has children clap or hop syllables at 6–8.
  - About 23 letter–sound links are enough (Ojanen 2015).
- **[Moderate]** Hyphenated text slowed first- and second-grade readers (Häikiö & Vainio 2018).
  - Use syllable splits only while a child is decoding, then drop them quickly.
- **[Moderate]** Teach Finnish letter *sounds*, never English letter names. Contrast the risky ones: a, e, i, y, j, ä, ö.

### Programmes

- **[Moderate–Strong]** GraphoGame (Ekapeli, Jyväskylä) adapts to keep about 80% success and trains letter → sound, then bigger units.
  - Struggling first-graders kept their lead into grade 3.
  - Second-language learners gained after 4–5 hours.
  - Its own researchers say "technology alone is not sufficient".
- **[Moderate]** Yle Pikku Kakkonen's songs and *Sanaleikki* are good to point families to; the app doesn't copy them.
- **[Moderate]** In heritage families, Finnish survives where parents decide firmly to use it, with identity as the reason (a study of Sweden Finnish families).
  - Only about one in four second-generation US children are fluent.
  - Expect children to understand far more than they can say.

## 3. The app compared with the evidence

### What already fits (protect it)

- Retrieval in every game, with no passive flashcards.
- Spaced repetition: Leitner boxes at 1/3/7/16 days, for words and for grammar (`con:` keys).
- Adaptive difficulty aimed at about 85% success, much like GraphoGame.
- Phrase frames instead of case tables.
- Wrong answers that teach; no fail states, lives or harsh sounds.
- A gentle streak with no loss warnings.
- Stories with a recurring cast, and tap-a-word meanings tied to the story.
- Acting things out: commands and *Kapteeni käskee*.
- Counting as number + noun (*kaksi koiraa*) from unit 3.
- Can-do statements for grown-ups.
- Speech recognition that is lenient and only gives bonus rounds.
- No leaderboards.

### Gaps (each one maps to a phase below)

| # | The evidence says | The app does | Phase |
|---|---|---|---|
| 1 | Phrases first; a rule noticed later and briefly, especially for the youngest | Every unit from unit 2 locks practice until an English rule lesson is read; the lessons aren't read aloud | 1, 2 |
| 2 | Lead with Finnish input | `BuildAPhrase`, `NameIt`, `ChooseForm` and `FindError` speak English first; English drops away only in Mestari, with no setting | 1 |
| 3 | Sessions of about 10–15 minutes, spaced and mixed, with a natural stop | Steps are endless streams; due review is reachable only from a home tile | 3 |
| 4 | About 6–10 meetings to understand a word, 15–20 before producing it; don't introduce close neighbours together | Nothing counts how often a word has been heard; only "at most 2 new words a round" | 3 |
| 5 | Hear the same story again and again, story first | The story is the unit's last step; one good read finishes it, and it comes back once | 2 |
| 6 | Social interaction and parent prompts have the biggest effects | Almost no co-play prompts | 4 |
| 7 | Speech recognition can't judge children | Lenient and bonus-only (good), but no recording to compare with | 4 |
| 8 | Length training, Finnish letter sounds and syllable steps | All of it sits in the optional Sounds corner; no syllables at all | 5 |
| 9 | Recognise everyday spoken Finnish | Spoken forms left out on purpose | 6 |
| 10 | Rewards should tell or surprise, not pay per answer | 1 star per right answer is the main reward | 7 |
| 11 | A good sound model | With no Finnish voice, Finnish is read in the browser's default (often English) voice | 1 |

**Decided against:**
- Changing the case order: the evidence is mixed and the current order is defensible.
- Songs: they wait for recorded audio.

### The four pillars, scored 0–3

| Pillar | Before | Target |
|---|---|---|
| Active (thinking, not just tapping) | 3 | 3 |
| Engaged (no distracting extras) | 2: per-answer stars, confetti | 3 |
| Meaningful (connected to the child's life) | 2: themes from an 8-year-old's life, but nothing linking screen to real life | 3 |
| Socially interactive | 0–1 | 2: together mode, try-at-home activities, hearing your own recording |

## 4. The plan (one pull request per phase)

1. **Who's learning, Finnish first, an honest voice.**
   - A reader band per child (younger/older), set by a grown-up.
   - An English help setting: always / fade / off.
   - English prompts on tap instead of spoken first.
   - No Finnish in a non-Finnish voice.
   - Explanations read aloud.
2. **Units restructured: hear it, use it, notice it.**
   - Each unit opens by listening to its story or scene.
   - The first practice step opens straight away.
   - The rule becomes a short "notice" step after practice. Younger readers get example and check cards only; older readers get the full lesson.
   - The story is read again with questions.
   - Stories return at 1, 3 and 7 days.
3. **The daily session.**
   - Continue builds a bounded session: due review, then new material, then something to finish on. It stops naturally.
   - A count of how often each word is heard; production waits for enough hearings.
   - New words from the same group never arrive together.
4. **Together mode.**
   - Prompt cards for the grown-up.
   - A try-at-home activity per unit, and a "this week" summary.
   - Record-and-compare speaking.
   - Links to real Finnish songs.
5. **Finnish sounds.**
   - A length track inside the course.
   - The letters that sound different from English.
   - Syllables for younger readers, from Wiktionary hyphenation, fading as the child decodes.
   - A test that no choice differs only in vowel harmony.
6. **Spoken Finnish to recognise.**
   - "Two ways to say it" cards, written by the native reviewer.
   - Mild spoken Finnish in child characters' lines.
7. **Rewards that tell.**
   - Round ends say what was learned.
   - A word book.
   - Occasional surprises.
   - The child's own 😀/😐/🙁 check on each can-do.
